# 4.7 South African context (additional stream)

**Research question.** What contextual factors should inform the design, implementation, and evaluation of a GenAI mental health intervention for young people in South Africa?

**Interpretation note.** Counts describe records and extraction coverage. Mentions from reviews, frameworks and non-South African studies are indirect evidence and are not treated as South African prevalence estimates.

**Direct preliminary answer.**
The directly relevant South African evidence was limited, but it identified several contextual issues that may shape uptake and safe implementation. These included stigma and fear of judgement, preference for human or blended support, shortages and geographic concentration of services, and the importance of privacy and anonymity (e.g., Humphries et al. (2026); Hazhir et al. (2025)). South African evidence also highlighted the need for local-language support, code-switching and options suitable for users with lower literacy, while review-mediated evidence identified gender- and identity-related differences in distress and access (e.g., Humphries et al. (2026); Hazhir et al. (2025); Humphries et al. (2026); Feng et al. (2025)). No strict primary record in this stream directly evaluated a GenAI mental-health intervention for young people in South Africa, so these findings should inform adaptation and testing rather than be treated as evidence that a particular design will work.

**Evidence base.**
We identified 6 records with information in at least one South African-context field. The contributing evidence comprised Evidence synthesis (n=5); Qualitative or mixed-methods (n=1). Settings were mixed (n=4); Not reported (n=1); other (n=1), and the technology or object types ranged from rule-based and conventional AI chatbots to GenAI or LLM systems and broader digital mental-health interventions. 6 records included South Africa, of which 1 provided direct South African primary evidence and 5 included South Africa through an evidence synthesis. Among all contributing records, 6 directly concerned adolescents or young people and 2 directly concerned GenAI or LLM systems. The record-level intersection of young people, GenAI and South Africa was n=2 (33.3%), but the stricter primary-study intersection was n=0 (0.0%). The latter is the more defensible measure of evidence directly matching MHAA.

**Key findings.**

**Help-seeking, stigma, service access and referral.**
Help-seeking, stigma, service access and referral. The most directly relevant South African qualitative evidence suggested that some users would prefer in-person counselling or use a chatbot as a supplement rather than a replacement. Privacy, anonymity and freedom from judgement were valued, particularly where stigma or discrimination discouraged disclosure and engagement. Access was also constrained by shortages of mental-health providers, concentration of services in urban areas, distance, cost and limited services tailored to marginalised groups (e.g., Humphries et al. (2026); Hazhir et al. (2025)). Referral was frequently discussed as an important function, but the extracted evidence provided limited detail on the capacity, availability or completion of referral pathways.

**Language, culture, literacy and population differences.**
Language, culture, literacy and population differences. South African records identified isiZulu and English use and called for support for isiZulu, isiXhosa and code-switching, including the ability to express emotions in the language that feels most natural. Voice-based interaction was suggested as one way to improve access for users with lower literacy or limited English (e.g., Humphries et al. (2026); Hazhir et al. (2025)). Evidence on gender and other differences was thinner. It included review-mediated examples concerning intimate-partner violence among young women and stigma affecting men who have sex with men and transgender women, but did not provide a comprehensive account of how gender, disability, rurality or socioeconomic position affect digital engagement (e.g., Humphries et al. (2026); Feng et al. (2025)). Named South African deployments were identified, but implementation lessons on adaptation, engagement and referral were often not reported in detail (e.g., Humphries et al. (2026); Feng et al. (2025)).

**Implications for MHAA.**
The evidence supports treating language, privacy, stigma and service availability as explicit design and implementation considerations rather than as background characteristics. MHAA should therefore test whether users can disclose distress comfortably in locally relevant language, including code-switching, and whether its privacy and anonymity messaging is understood by young people who may share devices or fear judgement. It should also specify what the intervention can and cannot provide and assess whether referral options are geographically accessible, affordable and able to accept users, rather than measuring referral only as a link or message being shown. These implications are grounded in the extracted South African evidence on stigma, language and service constraints (e.g., Humphries et al. (2026); Hazhir et al. (2025)). As a review-team interpretation, MHAA should co-design and pilot the language choices, disclosure flow and referral pathway with South African young people and service partners, retain a route to human support, and examine engagement and safety separately by language, gender, location and other relevant groups. The review does not establish that any one interface, language configuration or referral model is superior.

**Gaps and caveats.**
The evidence base was sparse and predominantly indirect. Only 1 contributing record provided direct South African primary evidence, and none met the strict primary-study definition of being simultaneously about young people, GenAI and South Africa. Field coverage was uneven: help-seeking patterns and gender-specific drivers were each populated in only 1 record(s), while language and service constraints were more commonly described. The extraction contained no robust South African reading-age estimate and limited South Africa-specific material on POPIA, SAHPRA, referral capacity or completion of referrals. Several findings came from reviews that mentioned South Africa without reporting the underlying study in detail, and records or reviews may overlap. Keyword tags, country flags and any record classified as direct on all three MHAA dimensions should therefore be checked against the supporting quotation and, where necessary, the source publication before the final section is drafted.
